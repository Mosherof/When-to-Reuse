Finding: Reinitialization Changes Dynamical Structure Despite Similar Task Performance
=====================================================================================
Date: March 3, 2026

Setup
-----
- 1-bit flip-flop task, batch_size=32, hidden_size=100, use_bias=True
- First training: 1000 epochs, lr=1e-2 → loss ≈ 0.001
- Reinitialization: W_in, W_out, b_out reinitalized with prob=1.0
- Second training: 1000 epochs, lr=1e-2 → loss ≈ 0.0006

Observation
-----------
After first training, the network consistently has 3 fixed points at every epoch
(two stable attractors near output ±1, one unstable at the origin). This is the
classic bistable flip-flop solution.

After full reinitialization and retraining, the network has only 1 true fixed point
(near the origin, output ≈ -0.021 ≈ 0), despite achieving even lower loss.

Diagnostic Details
------------------
First training (final epoch):
- 53232 seeds tested → 53232/53232 converge → 3 FPs
- Readouts: [-0.043] (saddle), [0.663] (stable), [-0.714] (stable)

Second training (final epoch):
- 5232 seeds tested (3232 trajectory + 2000 random at scales 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0)
- 4490/5232 seeds converge (energy < 1e-4), ALL to the same single fixed point
- Energy gap: true FP at ~1e-14, next lowest energy at ~1.3e-4
- 180 unique slow points found (energy 1e-4 to 0.1), but none cluster near ±1
- Slow point outputs are widely scattered, not concentrated at task-relevant values

Interpretation
--------------
                  | First training                          | After reinit + retrain
------------------|-----------------------------------------|----------------------------------
Fixed points      | 3 (two stable at ±1, one unstable at 0) | 1 (near origin)
Mechanism         | Classic bistable attractors             | Slow manifold / transient dynamics
Loss              | 0.001                                   | 0.0006

The reinitialized network solves the flip-flop task using continuous slow dynamics
along a manifold rather than discrete fixed-point attractors. The qualitative
dynamical structure changes fundamentally even when task performance is comparable
(or better).

Implication for "When to Reuse"
-------------------------------
Reinitializing input/output weights while preserving recurrent weights does not
preserve the fixed-point attractor landscape. The network finds a qualitatively
different dynamical solution, suggesting that the "reusable" computation encoded
in W_rec may be repurposed into a different dynamical regime by the new readout.

Could We Be Missing Fixed Points? (Validation Tests)
-----------------------------------------------------
Concern: The solver might fail to find FPs that genuinely exist.

Evidence against (from current diagnostics):
- 5232 seeds tested including random at 4 scales → all converge to the same FP
- 4490/5232 seeds converge (86%) → high convergence rate
- Energy gap is 10 orders of magnitude (1e-14 vs 1.3e-4) → no near-misses
- 500 GD steps + 20 Newton steps → generous optimization budget

Proposed validation tests:

1. DIRECT RESIDUAL CHECK (definitive, no optimization needed)
   Take the 3 FPs found during first training (r1*, r2*, r3*).
   Evaluate ||r* - tanh(r* W_rec' + b_rec')|| using the 2nd training's weights.
   If residual is near zero → FPs exist but solver fails (code bug).
   If residual is large → FPs genuinely don't exist under new weights.
   NOTE: W_rec and b_rec are NOT reinitialized, so this tests whether the
   reinitialization of W_in/W_out/b_out indirectly shifted FPs via training.

2. SEED FROM FIRST TRAINING'S FPs
   Use the 3 known FPs as explicit seeds in find_fixed_points_hybrid with
   the 2nd training's weights. If they converge to 3 distinct points, our
   random seeding strategy has a coverage problem.

3. WEIGHT INTERPOLATION (homotopy/continuation)
   Interpolate: W(λ) = (1-λ)W_first + λ*W_second for λ in [0, 1].
   Track FPs at each λ step. This reveals exactly where FPs appear/disappear
   (saddle-node or pitchfork bifurcations) and whether FPs merge or vanish.

4. BRUTE-FORCE SCALE TEST
   Run with 50k+ random seeds. If the count stays at 1, coverage is not
   the issue.

Priority: Test 1 first (simplest, most conclusive). If it shows large
residuals, the finding is confirmed. Tests 2-4 provide additional confidence.

Validation Results (Updated March 3, 2026)
--------------------------------------------
Using properly separated weight_snapshots1 (1st training) and weight_snapshots2
(2nd training) to avoid the earlier issue of using weight_snapshots2[0] as a
proxy for the first training's final weights.

All tests confirm: the missing FPs are genuinely destroyed, not missed by the solver.

Weight continuity check:
  W_rec gap (1st training final vs 2nd training epoch 0): 1.00
  b_rec gap (1st training final vs 2nd training epoch 0): 0.10
  → The very first gradient step of the 2nd training already shifts W_rec/b_rec.
  W_rec total change (1st final vs 2nd final): 9.92
  b_rec total change (1st final vs 2nd final): 0.63

Test 4 - Brute Force (53,232 seeds at 5 scales: 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0):
  1st training FINAL weights: 53232/53232 converge → 3 FPs
    Readouts: [-0.043], [0.663], [-0.714]
    (saddle near 0, two stable attractors at +0.66 / -0.71)
  2nd training FINAL weights: 40645/53232 converge → 1 FP
    Readout: [-0.024]
    (single near-origin FP)

Test 1 - Direct Residual Check (1st training's 3 FPs under both weight sets):
  FP#   Output     Resid(1st wts)   Resid(2nd wts)   Verdict
  1     [-0.043]   4.30e-08         6.96e-01         DESTROYED
  2     [ 0.663]   3.69e-07         7.86e+00         DESTROYED
  3     [-0.714]   3.95e-07         7.90e+00         DESTROYED
  → All 3 are genuine FPs under 1st training weights (residual ~1e-7),
    but all have massive residuals under 2nd training weights.

Test 2 - Seed from 1st training's FPs with 2nd training weights:
  Seeding from the 3 known FPs → converges to 1 FP (output=-0.024).
  The two stable attractors (±0.7) have no successor under the new weights.

Test 3 - Continuation / Homotopy (definitive, coverage-independent):
  Concern: Tests 1, 2, and 4 all rely on seeding the solver from candidate
  locations. In 100 dimensions, even 53k seeds is sparse — FPs with small
  basins of attraction could be missed regardless of seed count.

  Resolution: Interpolate W_rec and b_rec between 1st training's final
  weights (α=0) and 2nd training's final weights (α=1) in 100 steps.
  At each step, seed the solver from the PREVIOUS step's FPs plus small
  perturbations (scale 0.01). This tracks FPs as they continuously move
  through state space, independent of search coverage.

  Result:
    α = 0.000 → 0.051:  3 FPs (tracked from known ground truth)
    α = 0.061:           3 → 1 FPs  ← SADDLE-NODE BIFURCATION
    α = 0.061 → 1.000:  1 FP (stable through remaining 94% of interpolation)

  The two stable attractors (readouts ±0.7) are annihilated via a saddle-node
  bifurcation very early in the weight interpolation — only ~6% of the way
  from 1st to 2nd training weights. After that point, only the single
  near-origin FP exists in the entire state space.

  This is the strongest possible evidence: we directly observe the bifurcation
  event by continuously following each FP. The FPs don't drift to unreachable
  locations — they cease to exist.

Conclusion
----------
CONFIRMED — the 1st training produces a classic bistable flip-flop (3 FPs:
saddle + two attractors). After reinitialization of W_in/W_out/b_out and
retraining, W_rec/b_rec change by ||ΔW||=9.92, completely destroying the
bifurcation structure. The continuation test directly observes the saddle-node
bifurcation at α ≈ 0.06 — the two stable attractors are annihilated early
in the weight change, and no amount of further search can recover them because
they genuinely do not exist.

The network retains only 1 FP and solves the task via slow transient dynamics
along a manifold rather than discrete attractors. This qualitative change in
dynamical mechanism occurs despite comparable (or better) task performance,
indicating that the "reusable" recurrent computation is fundamentally
restructured during retraining.
